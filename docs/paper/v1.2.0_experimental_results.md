# Experimental Results for Monte Carlo DSL v1.2.0

## Experimental setup

The experimental evaluation was conducted using Monte Carlo DSL version v1.2.0. The evaluation focused on two aspects: statistical correctness and execution behavior under different worker configurations.

The statistical validation used analytical reference models with known theoretical means and variances. Each model was evaluated across five deterministic random seeds. The criterion for statistical agreement was whether the absolute estimation error fell within three Monte Carlo standard errors.

The benchmark evaluation measured execution time across two representative models: `normal_mean` and `heavy_expression`. Each configuration was executed ten times using 1, 2, and 4 requested workers, with 10,000, 100,000, and 1,000,000 iterations.

## Statistical validation

The statistical validation produced 20 validation rows across four analytical models: `normal_mean`, `uniform_mean`, `discrete_mean`, and `linear_combination`. All 20 validation rows satisfied the three-MCSE criterion, resulting in a validation pass rate of 100%.

This result indicates that the v1.2.0 statistical core reproduces the expected analytical behavior for the evaluated models. The validation also confirms that the reported statistical metadata, including Monte Carlo standard error, estimated variance, valid rate, discard rate, statistical core version, and random number generator metadata, is being produced consistently.

## Benchmark results

The benchmark evaluation produced 18 benchmark configurations. Of these, 12 involved multi-worker execution. Only 4 of the 12 multi-worker configurations produced a speedup greater than 1.0.

The best observed speedup was 1.039 for `normal_mean` with 10,000 iterations and 4 requested workers. However, this speedup is marginal and does not indicate strong parallel scalability.

For larger workloads, multi-worker execution frequently showed negative speedup. For example, in the `normal_mean` model with 1,000,000 iterations, mean execution time increased from 2.939 seconds with 1 worker to 3.324 seconds with 2 workers and 4.094 seconds with 4 workers. A similar pattern was observed for `heavy_expression`, where execution time increased from 3.064 seconds with 1 worker to 3.453 seconds with 2 workers and 4.548 seconds with 4 workers.

## Discussion

The statistical results provide strong evidence that the v1.2.0 statistical core behaves correctly for the tested analytical models. The use of deterministic seeds, explicit Monte Carlo standard error, and reproducible output files strengthens the reproducibility of the evaluation.

The benchmark results should be interpreted more cautiously. The tested local configuration does not provide evidence of strong parallel scalability. Instead, the results suggest that execution overhead dominates the computational cost of the evaluated models. This overhead may come from process management, generated-code execution, file I/O, interpreter startup cost, or worker coordination.

Therefore, the v1.2.0 results are best presented as a reproducibility and statistical-validation baseline rather than as evidence of optimized high-performance execution.

## Threats to validity

The evaluation was conducted on a single local environment, so the benchmark results may not generalize to other operating systems, processors, or runtime configurations.

The benchmark models are synthetic and may not represent all realistic Monte Carlo workloads. Heavier models or longer-running simulations may produce different scaling behavior.

The benchmark script currently exposes only a quick mode and a default full mode. Future versions should allow configurable iterations, repetitions, worker counts, and model selection from the command line.

## Conclusion

Monte Carlo DSL v1.2.0 establishes a reproducible statistical validation baseline. The statistical core successfully matches analytical expectations across the tested models. However, the current benchmark results do not support claims of strong parallel scalability. Future work should focus on reducing execution overhead, improving benchmark configurability, and evaluating heavier simulation workloads.
